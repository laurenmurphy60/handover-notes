## The problem

Marsh's "Sport" business covers national insurance programs for individual
players, clubs, and associations tied to a given sport — serviced day to day
by internal brokers and business partners. That servicing ran on legacy
websites scattered across multiple platforms and sports, each with its own
level of functionality, no shared information architecture, and no consistent
brand. The business had also been marked for strategic B2C growth, which
meant the rebuild had to do more than tidy up: it needed to consolidate
public insurance information, claims, and client servicing into one
experience that could scale to onboarding new sports without starting over
each time.

Before proposing a structure, I needed to answer four things: what our
internal stakeholders actually needed from the site, how they were engaging
with the legacy sites today, who was using the public site, and what those
people needed from us.

## What I changed

1. **Baselined the legacy estate first.** Rather than starting from opinions,
   I ran an in-depth evaluation of the 5 existing legacy sites across 9
   sports — sitemaps, thematic content analysis, functionality scope, and
   whatever Google Analytics and existing form/service data was available —
   to establish an honest baseline before proposing anything new.
2. **Ran the rebuild inside real constraints.** The new site had to sit on
   the existing Adobe Experience Manager platform, lean on existing
   design-system components with no new components for MVP, route any new
   functionality through a full development cycle, and ship within 6 months.
   The research had to produce something buildable inside that box, not a
   wish list.
3. **Used stakeholder workshops to surface assumptions, not solutions.** I
   designed sport-specific workshops around two goals — a high-level view of
   the business, and an honest map of internal versus external users — using
   a fixed set of open questions (who actually uses the site today, who
   *should* use it, what problem it solves, what differentiates us from
   competitors) so non-researchers could participate without needing to know
   the methodology.
4. **Synthesized by persona and task, not by sport.** After the workshops,
   I grouped findings by who was being served — individual players,
   clubs/associations, and brokers — and what they needed to get done,
   rather than by sport. That grouping became the basis for the site's user
   journeys.

## What changed as a result

The research reframed the entire scope of the rebuild:

- Information architecture across the legacy sites was inconsistent with no
  shared hierarchy, and general insurance content saw low engagement overall.
- Two actions accounted for the overwhelming majority of real engagement and
  inbound inquiries: **making a claim** and **requesting a Certificate of
  Currency**. Claims came mostly from players and club representatives;
  certificate requests came almost exclusively from club officials.
- That let us drop the assumption that each sport needed its own site or
  experience. Every sport could be served the same way — the new structure
  needed to be organized around the *audience* (broker, club/association,
  individual), with content depth split by group where club and player
  structures were more complex, not around which sport someone played.
- It also surfaced cross-selling as a capability the new site explicitly
  needed to support, not a nice-to-have.

## What I'd do differently

I was close to this business for an extended period, which meant a real risk
of tunnel vision — I'd have liked a way to validate the findings externally,
especially given how thin the existing analytics and data actually were.
Timeline and privacy/data constraints also ruled out surveying or
interviewing clubs, club members, and individual policyholders directly;
reaching the people we were actually serving, rather than relying on
stakeholder proxies for their needs, would have added a layer of insight the
workshops couldn't fully replace. And with the limited timeline, SEO research
stayed at a brief pass — a full keyword analysis would have made the content
strategy stronger going in.

**In short:** Off-market trade reporting is time critical. Participants can
have as little as 10 minutes to register a trade, and the legacy platform
made that hard. With limited design resources, design was focused on the
areas where it would make a meaningful difference: the core flows and the
customer journeys. Making the current features easy was the core piece. UX
became one of the programme's success stories and set the standard for how
design is embedded in delivery going forward.

I led the experience design direction for the modernisation of
business-critical off-market trade reporting. The work translated fragmented
customer journeys, manual processes and legacy system constraints into a
validated future-state product and service experience, connecting
end-to-end service design, customer co-design, detailed workflows and UI
patterns.

Rather than treating the programme purely as an interface redesign, the
approach was holistic. Current-state blueprints established the systemic
problems, customer workshops shaped the future direction, and iterative
usability testing helped de-risk the design before implementation.

### Scope

- Setting the experience design direction across service, UX and UI work
- Connecting customer needs to the broader platform transformation strategy
- Guiding the transition from current-state research into future-state concepts and detailed product workflows
- Establishing alignment across customer-facing and operational journeys
- Directing participant co-design and iterative product validation
- Shaping information architecture, interaction models and contextual navigation

- Derivatives Clearing Product

- Participant Transitions
- Clearing Operations

## The challenge

The off-market trade reporting platform supported the reporting and approval
of off-market derivatives trades. Over time, its customer and operational
experience had become constrained by ageing assets, fragmented access,
limited automation and incomplete digital workflows.

This was not an isolated usability problem. Research across the broader
clearing journey found recurring issues: manual processing, outdated
platforms, overuse of email, limited notifications, inaccessible
information, and workarounds caused by missing system functionality. These
conditions increased effort for participants and employees, and raised the
risk of errors in time-critical workflows.

At the platform level, customers and employees were dealing with:

- A paper-based access request requiring physical signatures, scanning and email submission
- Multiple administrative steps to set up firms, administrators and standard users
- Separate passwords and certificates across legacy systems
- Limited self-service for password and access management
- No account segregation between desks or groups within the same participant
- No mature role- or account-based authorisation model
- An unstable test environment supported by ageing assets
- Limited FIX coverage, with some trade types still requiring the web portal
- Insufficient documentation for technical, security and functional processes

The transformation therefore had to solve several interdependent problems at
once:

1. Replace a legacy experience without disrupting a regulated trading process
2. Reduce participant effort in time-constrained workflows
3. Improve operational visibility and exception management
4. Introduce stronger roles, permissions and authorisation patterns
5. Support both API-led and portal-led users
6. Provide a scalable experience foundation for future capability

## Participant pain points

### Manual, time-critical trade registration

Participants described trade entry as labour-intensive and prone to
data-entry errors. The interface required significant manual input, offered
no reusable templates for historical trades, and made bulk upload
inefficient in some circumstances. Depending on volume, participants could
spend several hours a day on registration, and strict time limits raised the
stakes of any friction:

- A 30-minute seller-entry window for EFPs
- A 10-minute seller-entry window for block trades
- A 60-minute buy-side EFP window
- Manual validation taking around three to six minutes per deal when required
- Participant concerns that interface limitations and manual steps made these cut-offs hard to meet

### Poor status visibility and limited notifications

The portal didn't provide reliable, real-time visibility of trade status.
Buyers could miss action windows because the platform didn't notify them
when a seller had registered a trade. Users repeatedly refreshed the page to
spot changes, while Trading Operations manually checked the system for
trades awaiting validation.

### Fragmented communication

Trade information moved through email, phone calls and multiple ticketing or
participant systems. This led to incomplete information, delayed
registration and weak auditability. ASX also had limited visibility of
counterparty communication before a transaction reached the platform.

### Weak correction and cancellation workflows

If a trade was entered incorrectly, participants couldn't simply edit or
cancel it without involving the counterparty or ASX, and the existing API
didn't support cancellation. Each cancellation incurred a $325 fee,
including cancellations after validation.

### Inadequate roles and access controls

The access model didn't support segregation between front-office and
back-office desks, and it lacked account-based permissions and mature role-
or notional-based authorisation. Participant feedback reinforced the need
for:

- Front-, middle- and back-office permissions
- Authoriser roles that could approve and cancel
- Bulk authorisation
- Maker/checker controls for manual entry
- API users capable of approving and cancelling
- A shared role and permissions model across the off-market trade reporting platform and the wider authorised-channel ecosystem

### Limited product and integration coverage

The API didn't support the complete product set. The GUI didn't support
multi-leg EFP reporting, and FIX supported only a subset of EFP trades, not
block trades. Customers had to work across multiple entry methods, which
limited straight-through workflows.

### Insufficient audit and reporting

Customers couldn't always retrieve reports when they needed them.
Historical or non-standard reports required help from internal teams,
customisation was limited, and long exports could lose data.

## Vision and strategy

The scope was defined as an end-to-end service transformation, connecting
the participant-facing product with onboarding, user access, operational
validation, reporting, communication and downstream clearing processes.
This allowed design decisions to be evaluated against the full service
rather than an isolated collection of screens.

The approach followed three connected modes:

1. Discover and analyse
2. Ideate and synthesise
3. Develop and test

Research sessions with subject-matter experts and existing customer feedback
were used to map the current experience from the outside in, creating a
current-state service blueprint.

### Research timeline

- **December 2023 – October 2024:** Core research workshops, followed by co-design workshops
- **October 2024:** First round of participant usability interviews and design playbacks
- **May 2025:** Second, scenario-based round of usability testing

The resulting customer journey, requirements and UX work became part of the
wider implementation plan for the replacement platform.

## Workshops as an alignment mechanism

Workshops ran across two rounds, 23 in total.

**Round one: core research (18 workshops)**

- 6 internal employee workshops
- 12 external customer workshops

**Round two: co-design (5 workshops)**

- 5 external participant workshops focused on off-market trade management, with a subset of participants from the core research

Together, these workshops surfaced recurring themes across notifications,
messaging, process tools, platforms, enriched trade details, reporting,
APIs, onboarding, permissions and self-management. The breadth of these
themes showed the opportunity couldn't be addressed through visual redesign
alone.

## Roles and permissions

The future direction replaced coarse user categories with a more deliberate
authorisation model. The focus was translating that technical access model
into understandable product behaviour:

- What a participant could see
- Which trades or accounts they could access
- Which actions they could initiate
- Which actions required another user
- How status and ownership should be communicated
- How access affected navigation, dashboards and actions

## Design system as infrastructure

The design system was treated as infrastructure rather than a styling layer:
a combination of standardised elements, principles, guidelines, components,
processes and documentation that creates consistency and efficiency. For
this platform, that meant reusable approaches for:

- Navigation and page context
- Tables and filtering
- Status indicators
- Pending-action patterns
- Validation, warning and error states
- Forms and multi-leg trade entry
- Approval controls
- Modals and confirmation messages
- Audit and history presentation

## Key decisions and trade-offs

### Prioritising core flows

The focus was on the key features rather than new or additional ones.
Making the current features easy to use was the core piece.

### Interface users first

The interface was the business continuity path for API users. In a
time-critical situation, it's the most important experience to get right.

### Meaningful error states over long flows

Rather than long, guided flows, the priority was clear and meaningful error
states at the point of action.

### A steeper learning curve over a slow process

People are in the platform every day. A steeper learning curve was a better
trade-off than a slow process.

## Leading the work

### Design influence at the right points

The programme was part of a larger piece of work with limited resources.
Design was focused on the most high-impact areas, where it would make a
meaningful difference: in-depth work on the customer journeys, and a light
touch on the process maps.

### Representing design where decisions are made

This meant representing design at key forums, talking through the design
process, and aligning with the wider group.

### Creating the right conditions for the product designer

Setting expectations early about what design would need and where it needed
to be included:

- Working closely with front-end engineering
- Access to customers to test with
- Prototypes and UAT environments for testing
- Additional support to capture customer feedback in real time

## Validating and de-risking the work

### Testing round one

- 4 usability interviews
- Participants from 3 companies
- High-, medium- and low-volume users
- 3 participant playback forums: an in-person Sydney session, a Teams showcase and a Zoom showcase
- Review of onboarding, user access, trade reporting, APIs, the dashboard and trade-entry wireframes

The overall response to the initial concepts was positive, and testing
exposed important differences between portal and API users. Portal users
understood the separation between actions and recent trades more quickly.
API users took longer but adjusted to the concept. Participants consistently
valued descriptive status language over system codes.

The testing led directly to design recommendations, including:

- Renaming the primary dashboard concept to "Pending Actions"
- Improving status descriptions
- Introducing more flexible filtering
- Supporting user preferences for column order
- Exploring faster approval without hiding necessary trade detail
- Clarifying notification and messaging behaviour

### Testing round two

The second round involved six one-on-one interviews across six participant
companies. Participants ranged from low-volume users completing zero to
seven trades through to high-volume users handling more than 100 trades on
busy days. The scenarios covered the dashboard, trade audits, trade entry,
warnings, bulk upload and cancellation.

This round confirmed several strengths:

- Most participants found Bulk Upload and Create New Trade easy to locate
- The trade-entry screen was considered intuitive and well designed
- All participants welcomed the detailed audit-trail concept
- The entry point for Cancel and Replace was intuitive for most participants
- The replacement-approval flow felt similar to a standard approval process

It also exposed issues to resolve:

- Participants couldn't always distinguish their own actions from counterparty actions
- Trade ID needed greater visibility
- Some warnings looked like hard errors
- Bulk-upload messaging didn't clearly identify incomplete entries
- The relationship between original, cancelled and replacement trades was unclear
- New capabilities such as detailed audit trails and Cancel and Replace needed onboarding and education

## Final solution

### 1. Action-led dashboard

The dashboard was designed to foreground work requiring attention, rather
than operate as a passive list of trades. It prioritised:

- Pending actions
- Status and ownership
- Time-sensitive trades
- Search and filtering
- Manual trade creation
- Access to trade history and audit information

The primary rationale was to reduce the time needed to work out "what do I
need to do next?", particularly for users managing high trade volumes or
strict cut-offs.

### 2. Simplified trade entry

Trade entry was redesigned to support:

- Block and EFP trade types
- Multiple futures and physical legs
- Manual and bulk workflows
- More compact presentation of information
- Pre-population where appropriate
- Visible market-price ranges
- Duplicate warnings
- Internal references
- Clearer dates, labels and terminology

Participant feedback supported selecting the trade type from the dashboard,
visible range information, contract names and easier access to bulk upload.

### 3. Validation at the point of action

The future-state platform introduced real-time validation during trade
entry. The design distinguished between:

- Hard errors that prevent progression
- Warnings that allow progression with acknowledgement
- Duplicate or rule-related alerts
- Clear location of the affected field or trade
- Explanatory language that supports corrective action

### 4. Maker/checker and role-aware approvals

The future-state experience incorporated maker/checker authorisation and
user administration, responding to compliance and operational concerns with
the existing platform. Approvals were designed as part of the underlying
transaction state rather than a disconnected administrative section. This
preserved trade context while making the required person, action and next
state more visible.

### 5. Audit and history

The platform direction included detailed audit trails and up to two years of
accessible trade history. Participants wanted to understand:

- Who submitted or modified a trade
- When a modification occurred
- How a trade moved through its status history
- Who approved it
- How a cancelled trade related to its replacement

### 6. Cancel and Replace

The new Cancel and Replace flow aimed to remove a manual, support-dependent
process by letting participants start cancellation and replacement within
the platform. The entry point tested well, but the experience needed
stronger visual differentiation between original, cancelled and replacement
records. Recommendations included highlighting modified values, and
improving status language and the relationship between linked trades.

### 7. Unified API and interface capability

The target direction aimed to unify more API and in-app functionality,
reducing the capability gap between users who integrate directly and those
who rely on the portal.

## Outputs and impact

### Influence on the programme

UX was recognised as a success and a key part of delivering the programme.
The design approach was embedded into the delivery framework, setting the
standard for projects going forward.

### Outputs

An evidence-backed future-state direction that connected service
transformation to capabilities the platform could deliver:

- A mapped current-state service across participant and employee touchpoints
- A redesigned dashboard and trade-entry experience
- Defined maker/checker and role-based workflow requirements
- Real-time validation patterns
- Detailed audit and reporting concepts
- An in-platform Cancel and Replace workflow
- Stronger alignment between portal and API capabilities
- Reusable design-system patterns for the new experience
- Two rounds of customer validation feeding directly into design refinement

### De-risking change

Design reduced transformation risk and built delivery confidence by:

- Converting fragmented customer and operational evidence into a shared future-state model
- Testing high-risk workflows before implementation
- Revealing ambiguous warnings, statuses and record relationships before production use
- Validating the discoverability of major actions such as Bulk Upload, audit access and Cancel and Replace
- Establishing reusable platform patterns instead of screen-by-screen inconsistency
- Connecting customer expectations with roles, permissions, APIs and downstream technical constraints
- Creating a clearer design basis for requirements, architecture, build and testing

## Measuring success

**Baseline:** Participants spent up to several hours a day on trade
registration, manual validation took around three to six minutes per deal,
and each cancellation cost $325 and required support.

Metrics to be measured post-launch:

- Reduction in manual trade-cancellation contacts
- Reduction in participant setup time
- Reduction in Trading Operations validation effort
- Task-completion improvement for trade entry and approval
- Reduction in support tickets and password or access requests
- Change in data-entry and trade-submission errors
- Design-system component adoption rate
- Improvement in design-to-development cycle time
- Post-launch CSAT or participant-effort score
- Commercial or operational cost avoidance

## Reflection

- **Earlier involvement:** Involving design earlier in the programme
- **More testing:** Pushing for more prototyping and usability testing
- **Wider playback:** More exposure and playback to the business outside the project

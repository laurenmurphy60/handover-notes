**In short:** It's rarely a roadmap, and usually dissonance. ASX was
organised around functional teams, but customers experienced it as a whole.
This discovery mapped the end-to-end experience across three customer
segments, front stage and backstage, for the first time. The blueprints
shifted the conversation from fixing individual applications to investing
in shared capabilities, with unified digital identity emerging as the
highest-leverage priority in the subsequent digital strategy.

## How do you know transformation is needed?

It's rarely a roadmap, and usually dissonance.

After spending a good amount of time getting to know the business, the
people, its capabilities and the way work was delivered, it became clear the
organisation was structured around functional teams within organisational
boundaries. The customer journeys were the opposite: intricate, and crossing
multiple teams, products, technologies and decision-makers.

The organisation delivered within functions, but customers experienced it as
a whole. That disconnect between how the business operated and how customers
experienced it was the signal that transformation was needed.

## There was no lack of research, just no way to put it together

Surprisingly, there was plenty of research:

- Four years' worth of customer insight
- A collaboration with an external partner
- A customer portal mid-build
- A series of feedback sessions spanning 18 different customers

Every piece had been done in isolation, to solve a problem or tick a box,
not to reach a common goal. None took a human-centred approach to the
experience of customers and employees together.

The same pattern showed up in delivery. Product owners, subject matter
experts and operational teams were solving requirements within their own
domains. Each solution made sense on its own, but the cumulative result was
duplicated capability, fragmented ownership, inconsistent data, multiple
customer channels and a growing number of short-term fixes.

So the challenge wasn't "how might we improve an existing portal?" It was:

> How might we create a shared understanding of ASX's end-to-end services,
> so the organisation can tell the difference between local improvements and
> the enterprise capabilities needed for lasting change?

## Shaping a programme: "It's not like the last time"

That line got repeated a lot by the design team. Many stakeholders had been
through discovery before. They'd been sold a dream, given up hours of their
time, and then watched the work get put on a shelf. That doesn't make for
enthusiastic stakeholders.

So the programme needed a clear scope, a clear purpose and clear outputs
that would demonstrate the value of bringing everything together. Focusing
on key journeys connected to commercial growth created a lean, repeatable
instrument that could be built on and extended.

Scope wasn't decided by convenience or the loudest request. It was shaped by
several lenses together:

- Where customer journeys began and ended
- Which teams and portfolios owned each part of the experience
- Existing customer feedback and survey evidence
- Areas with significant complaints, areas with positive feedback, and areas with no customer signal at all
- Revenue-generating parts of the business
- Areas with significant operational cost
- Known technology degradation and risk
- Opportunities relevant to more than one business unit

Instead of treating the organisation as a list of applications, the scope
was organised around three customer ecosystems: Issuers, Participants, and
Technology & Data.

## Being uncomfortable

Discovery into a manual process is never a simple or kind journey.

When you ask a team to walk you through how they actually do something,
including the workaround, the file saved to desktop, the spreadsheet, and
the email trail that isn't technically part of the process but still
happens, you're basically asking them to document their own shortcomings and
inefficiencies. And the word "transformation" often travels alongside
"restructure" and "automation."

So the most significant engagement challenge was this: how do you get the
information you need, in the right way, by creating a safe environment where
people will talk openly about their operations?

The goal was a space where stakeholders could:

- Describe where processes were breaking down
- Critique systems without fear of personal consequence
- Explain undocumented workarounds
- Surface tensions between departments
- Acknowledge where customer experiences were failing
- Equally, defend processes that were necessary or working well

Two things made this work.

First, the employee experience carried the same weight as the customer
experience. The service blueprints capture both in parallel, at every stage
of the journey.

Second, findings and critiques were only ever framed at a systems level.
Four themes came up again and again: absence of a centralised view, manual
processes, inconsistent data capture, and inadequate user experience. These
are all statements about architecture and systems, not about people.

Participation was deliberately broad. It included people who already trusted
design, teams who had little previous involvement with design, SMEs embedded
in operational detail, portfolio owners, and the people behind systems,
data, billing, compliance and support. This reduced the risk of reinforcing
a single established narrative.

### Numbers over noise

Almost every qualitative finding was paired and weighted with real numbers
where they existed. This helped take the loudest voices and inherent bias
out, and stopped the findings from being dismissed as anecdotal.

## Taking stakeholders on the journey

A meaningful amount of time went into explaining why this time was
different, and what design actually is: what service design is, how it
relates to product design and process mapping, how to read a service
blueprint, and why it helps build the right thing.

A lot of that material came to life because the audience needed it. Design
maturity across the organisation was uneven, and the work could only carry
weight with people who understood how and why it was done.

The team didn't disappear into discovery and come back with a final answer.
Work was played back regularly and in smaller pieces, so stakeholders could
challenge interpretations and add missing context. This did three things:

- **Protected accuracy:** Complex operational detail was corrected before it became embedded in the final artefacts.
- **Maintained trust:** Stakeholders could see how their input was being used.
- **Built ownership:** The blueprints became a shared picture of the organisation, not a critique imposed by design at the end.

## What the blueprints showed

### Customers lacked continuity across the journey

Becoming and remaining an ASX-listed entity involved many stages, teams,
systems, documents, approvals and payments, but it wasn't managed as one
service. Prospective issuers struggled to find tailored information. Once an
application was in assessment, they had limited visibility of its status and
relied on emailing different teams for updates. Technology & Data customers
moved between Business Development, Connectivity Support, Customer Technical
Services, Information Services, Financial Operations and others, with no
unified view of progress or ownership.

### Manual processes pushed organisational complexity onto customers

Issuer applications could involve oversized documents split across multiple
emails, separate accounts-receivable forms, manual payments and repeated
customer data. Technology & Data customers ordering several products could
complete multiple order forms and agreements containing the same
information, which was then manually re-entered into the CRM and passed
downstream.

### Identity was built around systems, not people

Third-party company secretaries working across several entities could need
multiple usernames and passwords, signing into separate company accounts to
manage users and details. Passwords for the Companies portal expired every
45 days, adding to the burden for customers juggling several identities.

### There was no single source of truth

Customer information moved manually across eight different systems and
channels during Issuer onboarding. Discrepancies between the CRM and finance
systems meant employees reconciled data by hand, and inaccurate data could
contribute to incorrect billing.

### Compliance lacked consistency and transparency

Regulated information arrived as structured online forms, unstructured PDFs,
Word documents converted to PDF, emails and, in some cases, faxes. Customers
were unsure which pathway applied or where their submission sat. Market
Announcement Officers reviewed lengthy documents for minor errors, and
published announcements couldn't simply be edited, creating extra submission
and deletion workflows for corrections.

## The outputs

Three current-state service blueprints, one per segment, each mapping the
end-to-end experience of key commercial journeys from the outside in: what
the customer does, what forms they see, who they speak to and how they feel
about it, based on real feedback. Alongside that, what employees are doing,
which teams truly own processes, and where support sits.

**Issuers:** An end-to-end customer journey across ten stages (discover,
research, application, admission and onboarding, company listed, marketing
and engagement, announcements, compliance, enquiries, offboarding), with
detailed blueprints for:

- Listings core journey
- ETF product issuer journey
- Market announcements
- Legal and compliance
- Customer lifecycle management
- Marketing and value-added services

**Technology & Data:** Blueprints across discovery through to offboarding,
covering:

- Connectivity Services
- Information Services distributors
- Information Services subscribers
- ASX Energy data services

## Short-term delivery vs long-term investment

The biggest tension throughout was short-term delivery needs versus
long-term strategic investment.

Within one delivery silo, rebuilding an application or buying a dedicated
tool can look like the practical answer. The blueprints showed why that was
often incomplete. A form builder bought for one application could support
structured data capture across many services. An identity solution for one
portal could solve authentication, security, privacy and support problems
across multiple channels.

This gave the organisation a clearer way to separate:

- **Immediate remediation:** Targeted changes that reduce customer or employee pain now
- **Reusable capability investment:** Where several services share the same underlying need
- **Strategic transformation:** Where lasting improvement depends on identity, data, integration, workflow and platform change

This wasn't an argument against short-term delivery. It was a way to stop
tactical work from adding long-term complexity.

## What rose to the top

### 1. Unified digital identity

Identity was the highest-leverage recommendation because it connected to so
many of the organisation's biggest concerns: customer convenience,
authentication, authorisation, cybersecurity, privacy, delegated access,
customer support, cross-channel consistency, and a coherent customer view.

The discovery showed users couldn't be modelled as accounts inside
individual applications. One person might work for one company, act for
several others, manage multiple entities or instruments, and hold different
permissions in each. The recommendation wasn't "add single sign-on." It was
to establish an identity, organisation, relationship, role and permission
model that could support a broader digital ecosystem.

### 2. Standardised data capture

Customers repeatedly entered the same information across forms,
applications and agreements, and employees then re-keyed, reconciled and
reformatted it. The opportunity was to move from document-centred
submissions to structured, validated data collection that could prefill
known information, validate earlier, reduce repeated questions, improve data
quality and enable automation.

## The ultimate output

### Influence on the digital strategy

The discovery became part of how ASX understood its digital future. The
subsequent Digital Strategy included a customer platform supporting
connected customer insights and unified digital identity; a compliance
platform focused on automation, augmentation and transparency; and digital
data collection built on a consistent data model. Unified identity,
self-service onboarding, channel convergence, digital validation and journey
optimisation became connected focus areas.

### Enabling the unified identity programme

Unified identity moved beyond an experience recommendation into architecture
and governance. The later customer identity programme addressed fragmented
identity storage, inconsistent authorisation, limited single sign-on and
inconsistent MFA, with a target state of secure authentication, rationalised
channels and a unified experience. The discovery didn't own that programme.
Its contribution was establishing identity as a high-impact customer and
enterprise problem, with the service evidence to back it.

### Better roadmap decisions

Product owners, designers and management could now test a proposed change
against the whole service, not just one application:

- How many journey stages does it affect?
- Does the pain exist across customer segments?
- Does it reduce customer effort, employee effort, or both?
- Is it a symptom or the underlying cause?
- Is similar investment happening elsewhere?
- Could it become a reusable capability?

### Uplifting design maturity

The blueprints gave designers a foundation for every engagement that
followed. Instead of starting with one requirement or one SME's view, they
could draw on the full picture: customer lifecycles, operational teams,
processes, systems, dependencies and known pain points.

That changed the questions design could ask:

- Is this a local need, or part of an enterprise pattern?
- Are we solving the customer problem, or only changing the interface?
- What happens backstage when the customer completes this action?
- Which system owns the information?
- Are we improving the service, or adding another isolated solution?

It also changed how the business saw design: as a function that could work
in complex, regulated domains, respect deep expertise, facilitate honest
conversations and turn evidence into strategic direction. That trust created
the basis for design's later involvement in strategy, identity, onboarding
and platform concepts.

The blueprints also became organisational memory. They captured knowledge
that had lived in people's heads, informal workarounds and undocumented
processes, and they continue to support ideation, onboarding new team
members and prioritisation.

## Reflection

Engineering should have been involved much earlier and more deeply. The
blueprints gave a strong view of customer experience, employee experience,
processes and opportunities, but a closer engineering partnership would have
added depth on system integrations, architectural dependencies, technical
debt and the risk of maintaining the current state.

That would have shown customer pain, employee effort, business risk and
technology risk with equal strength, and helped the recommendations land
with a wider executive audience. Fragmented identity and manual
reconciliation aren't only experience problems. They're also security,
privacy and operational risks.

The strongest strategic decisions happen when design, product, the business
and engineering build the evidence together, from the start.
